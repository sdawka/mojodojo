When I first arrived in the valley in early March, the river was still locked beneath a crust of grey ice, and the only sound in the mornings was the occasional crack of the surface shifting as the temperature climbed above freezing for a few hours each afternoon. The village at the mouth of the valley had perhaps two hundred residents, most of whom made their living from sheep, timber, and a small hydroelectric station built in the nineteen-fifties. My task was simple to describe and difficult to carry out: measure the flow of the river at six points along its length, sample the water chemistry every week, and build a model that could predict how the valley would respond to the heavier spring storms that the regional climate projections had begun to forecast.

The first problem was access. The upper three gauging stations could only be reached on foot, along a path that climbed nearly six hundred meters over four kilometers and was, for the first two months, buried under snow that reached my waist in the drifts. I learned quickly to start before dawn, when the snow was firm enough to walk on, and to be back below the tree line before the sun softened the slope. The instruments themselves were older than I was. Two of the pressure transducers had to be replaced in April after meltwater flooded their housings, and the data logger at the highest station recorded nothing at all for eleven days because a marmot had chewed through its cable.

By late May the river had become unrecognizable. The gentle channel I had photographed in March was now a brown, roaring torrent that carried whole tree trunks past the village bridge. The peak discharge, which I recorded on the twenty-sixth of May, was nearly forty times the winter base flow. What surprised me most was not the size of the flood but its timing: the peak arrived almost two days after the warmest weather, delayed by the time it took meltwater to percolate through the deep layer of glacial till that covers the valley floor. This lag, it turned out, was the key to the entire model. The soil acted as a sponge, absorbing the first surge of water and releasing it slowly, which meant that the villagers had far more warning of a flood than anyone had assumed.
